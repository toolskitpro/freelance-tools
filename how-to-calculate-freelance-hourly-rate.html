Why Most Freelancers Undercharge

Before we dive into numbers, let us understand why so many freelancers struggle with pricing:

  • They only consider their salary goal — forgetting taxes, software, insurance, and vacation
  • They compare themselves to employees — but freelancers pay self-employment tax and get no paid leave
  • They fear losing clients — so they price based on fear, not value
  • They do not track non-billable hours — admin, proposals, and emails eat 30-40% of your time

The Formula: How to Calculate Your Rate

Here is the simple but powerful formula every freelancer should use:

Hourly Rate = (Annual Salary Goal + Annual Expenses + Annual Tax) ÷ Total Billable Hours

Let us break this down step by step with real numbers.

Step 1: Set Your Annual Salary Goal

How much do you want to take home after all expenses and taxes? Be honest with yourself. If you were an employee, what salary would make you comfortable?

  • US Example: $80,000/year
  • UK Example: £50,000/year
  • Canada Example: C$75,000/year

Step 2: Calculate Your Working Weeks

You are not a robot. You need time off. Subtract vacation, sick days, and holidays:

  • Total weeks in a year: 52
  • Vacation: 4 weeks
  • Sick days + holidays: 2 weeks
  • Working weeks: 46

Step 3: Estimate Your Billable Hours

Most freelancers can only bill 20-30 hours per week. The rest goes to admin, marketing, proposals, and client calls.

Step 4: Add Your Business Expenses

Freelancers have costs employees do not think about:

  • Software subscriptions (Adobe, Figma, VS Code, etc.)
  • Internet and phone bills
  • Coworking space or home office setup
  • Health insurance
  • Equipment (laptop, monitor, etc.)
  • Professional development (courses, books)

Typical monthly expenses: $300-$800 depending on your field.

Step 5: Factor in Taxes

This is where most freelancers get shocked. Self-employment tax is higher than employee tax because you pay both portions.

Real Example: US Freelance Designer

Let us put it all together for a freelance designer in the US:

  • Desired salary: $80,000
  • Annual expenses: $6,000 ($500/month)
  • Tax rate: 30%
  • Working weeks: 46
  • Billable hours/week: 25
  • Total billable hours: 1,150

Calculation:

Total needed before tax = ($80,000 + $6,000) × 1.30 = $111,800

Hourly Rate = $111,800 ÷ 1,150 = $97/hour

Add 20% buffer for non-billable time = $117/hour

When to Raise Your Rates

Your rate is not set in stone. Raise it when:

  • You have 6+ months of consistent client work
  • Clients are saying "yes" too quickly (you are too cheap)
  • You have learned new skills or gained certifications
  • It has been 12 months since your last increase
  • Your expenses have increased (software, living costs)

Common Pricing Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Copying competitors blindly — Their costs and skills are different from yours
  2. Not including taxes in your rate — You will owe thousands at year-end
  3. Charging the same rate for all clients — Complex projects deserve higher rates
  4. Not tracking your actual hours — You might be earning less than minimum wage
  5. Giving discounts too easily — It devalues your work

FAQ

Should I charge hourly or per project?

Both have pros and cons. Hourly is safer for unclear scope. Fixed-price earns more if you are efficient. Many freelancers use a hybrid: hourly for discovery, fixed for delivery.

What if clients say my rate is too high?

That is a filtering mechanism, not a problem. Clients who cannot afford your rate were never your target. Focus on clients who value quality over cheapness.
