Why most freelancers undercharge

The number one reason freelancers lose money is not low skills - it is guessing the price. When you quote by feeling instead of by numbers, you almost always quote too low. Clients rarely complain about a price that is too low, so the mistake only shows up later when the project takes three times longer than expected.

The fix is simple: break every project into numbers before you name a price. This guide shows you exactly how.

Step 1: Break the project into tasks

Never estimate a whole project in one number. List every task separately:

  • Discovery call and requirements (1-2 hours)
  • Actual production work (design, coding, writing)
  • Revisions and client feedback rounds
  • Communication, meetings and emails
  • File delivery and documentation

Most freelancers count only the production work and forget the rest. Communication alone often takes 20-30% of total project time.

Step 2: Estimate hours per task

Give each task a realistic hour estimate, then add them up. Now apply one rule: multiply your total by 1.3. Projects almost always run longer than expected. That 30% buffer is not greed - it is accuracy.

Step 3: Multiply by your real hourly rate

Your rate must cover your salary, taxes, software, hardware and unpaid time (holidays, sick days, admin work). If you do not know your real hourly rate yet, use our Hourly Rate Calculator before quoting anything.

Step 4: Adjust for complexity

Not all hours are equal. Add a complexity multiplier:

  • Standard project: clear requirements, familiar work - no change
  • Medium complexity: unclear scope, some research needed - add 15%
  • High complexity: new technology, vague brief, difficult client - add 30%

Step 5: Add revisions, expenses and rush fees

Three things freelancers forget to charge for:

  1. Revisions: define how many rounds are included. Extra rounds cost money - say it in the quote.
  2. Expenses: stock photos, plugins, paid tools - pass these to the client at cost or with a small markup.
  3. Rush work: a deadline that forces you to work nights or weekends deserves 15-50% extra.

Step 6: Structure the payments

Never do 100% of the work before seeing any money. The safest structure for most projects:

  • 40% upfront deposit - work starts only after this is paid
  • 40% at a mid-project milestone - after a demo or first draft
  • 20% on final delivery - sent before you hand over final files
If a client refuses even a small deposit, that is a warning sign. Serious clients pay deposits without argument.

Step 7: Add a negotiation buffer

Quote 10-20% above your calculated minimum. If the client accepts immediately - great. If they negotiate, you have room to give a small discount while still hitting your real minimum.

A quick example

A web design project estimated at 40 hours, rate $35/hour:

  • Base: 40 x $35 = $1,400
  • Complexity (medium): +15% = $1,610
  • Revisions buffer: +$120
  • Stock images and fonts: +$60
  • Rush fee (hard deadline): +25% = $447
  • Minimum quote: $2,237 - quote $2,500 to the client

What to write in the actual quote

Keep it short and clear:

  1. What is included (scope, deliverables, number of revisions)
  2. What is NOT included (this prevents endless scope creep)
  3. Timeline with milestones
  4. Price and payment structure (40/40/20)
  5. Validity - quotes expire after 14 days

Final tip

Quoting is a skill. Your first five quotes will feel uncomfortable, and that is normal. Every time a project finishes, compare your quote with the real hours you worked, and adjust your next quote. Within a few months your estimates will become sharp.
